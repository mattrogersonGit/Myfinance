Teaching Kids About Earning

"One of the most important money lessons children can learn is that money usually comes from somewhere."

Children benefit from understanding the connection between effort, work, earning and choice. Money doesn't simply appear.

There's no one right way

Pocket money vs earned money

Some families give regular pocket money regardless of chores. Others link some or all pocket money to jobs. Others use a combination — there isn't one correct system.

Our own approach: regular household responsibilities, plus opportunities to earn additional money from bigger jobs. See it in full on Our 3-Jar Money System.

Just part of being in a family

Everyday responsibilities

Not every household task needs to be paid — children are part of a family and can reasonably be expected to contribute. Keep the exact list age appropriate.

Extra jobs

Additional jobs — washing the car, mowing lawns, gardening, cleaning windows — are different from normal responsibilities. The child can decide whether the extra money is worth the effort.

Washing the car Mowing lawns Gardening Cleaning windows Other larger jobs

This introduces an important economic idea: is the reward worth the work?

The bigger idea

Connect work to value

Earning money isn't simply about being paid for moving around — people earn money because they provide something useful or valuable. For children this can be simplified: "you do something that someone else values, and they give you money in return."

Washing someone's car Mowing a lawn Selling something they made Helping with a task
A real decision

Let children choose

$5"You can earn $5 by washing the car."

The child decides whether they want to spend the time doing it — helping them understand opportunity cost and the relationship between time, effort and money.

For older children

Starting a small business

Simple ideas like selling handmade items, lawn mowing, washing cars, or helping neighbours with appropriate jobs introduce identifying a need, providing something useful, charging a price, earning revenue, and understanding costs — kept introductory, not a business plan.

Both have a place

Earning is not the same as free money

There's a difference between "here is $5" and "you completed this job and earned $5." Both can be useful in a child's financial education — the second introduces the connection between effort and reward. That doesn't mean parents should never give children money without work.

Once it's earned

What to do with earned money

Earning is only the beginning. Once children earn money, bring them back to the three choices — Spend, Save, Give — because that's where the real financial decision-making starts.

Spending Saving Giving
An honest conversation

Teaching fairness

Not every job should have the same payment. Some jobs take longer, require more effort, require more skill, or create more value — a good introduction to the idea that prices and wages aren't simply based on how long something takes. Keep it age appropriate.

Parent callout

If children are paid for every single thing they do, they may start seeing family responsibilities as transactions. It's reasonable to have some jobs that are simply part of being a family member, while extra work provides an opportunity to earn.

Try it this weekend

A simple challenge

Choose one extra job

Before starting, agree:

  • What needs to be done
  • What "finished" looks like
  • How much it will pay

After the job:

  • Pay the child
  • Let them count the money
  • Decide where it goes

Then ask: was the money worth the work?
