Money Trade-offs

Choosing one thing with your money usually means not choosing something else.

The basics

What is a trade-off?

When you choose to spend money one way, you can't spend that same money another way. That's not a bad thing — it's just how choices work. Trade-offs are simply part of deciding what to do with limited money.

You have $20. You could buy a $20 toy today. Or you could save that $20 toward something that costs $50. You can't do both with the same $20.

A real scenario

Three ways to use $10

Option A

Spend $6 on a toy, have $4 left.

Option B

Spend $3 on a treat, save $7.

Option C

Save all $10.

There's no universally correct choice here — what matters is understanding what each option actually means for you.

When it gets trickier

Trade-offs with needs and wants

Trade-offs become more important when there's something you need as well as something you want. You have $10. You need something costing $7. You want something costing $5. What choices do you actually have?

Saving is a trade-off too

Choosing to wait is still a choice

Saving isn't really "not spending" — it's its own trade-off. You give up spending your money today, in exchange for having more available later.

Try it yourself

Then talk about what you chose — and what you decided not to do with that money instead.

For parents

A useful question to ask: "If we spend the money on this, what else could we do with it?" Try to avoid framing choices as right or wrong — the goal is helping your child think through the consequences, not judging the decision itself.

For older children

There's actually a word for this: opportunity cost — what you give up when you choose something else. It's an optional extra idea, not the main point of this lesson.
