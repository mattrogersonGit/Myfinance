Before anything else — what money actually is, and what it can do.

The basics

What is money?

Money is something people use to buy things and pay for services. Instead of swapping a toy for a loaf of bread, people use money — coins and notes — to trade for what they need or want.

The best place to start is with money you can actually hold: coins and notes. You can see them, touch them, count them, save them, spend them, and give them away. That's real, physical money — and it's the easiest way to understand how money works.

Four things

What can money do?

Every dollar you have can do one of these things.

💸

Spent

Traded for something you want.

🏺

Saved

Kept for something later.

🎁

Given

Shared with someone else.

💪

Earned

Received for doing a job.

An important idea

Why can't we buy everything we want?

Because there's only so much money to go around. Everyone — kids and grown-ups — has a limited amount of money, so we all have to make choices about what to buy and what to skip.

That's really what this whole lesson is building toward: money is limited, so what you do with it matters.

Counting it up

Money has value

Every coin and note is worth a certain amount — and you can count it to know exactly how much you have.

$1
one $1 coin
$5
one $5 note
$5
five $1 coins

Five $1 coins add up to $5 — the same as one $5 note. It's not about how many coins you have, it's about what they add up to.

Try it yourself

Think about it

Have a go at these before checking the answer.

You have $5. You spend $2. How much do you have left?

You have $3 left. Spending money means you have less of it — that's the trade.

You have $5. You could buy a $4 toy, or save the $5 for something bigger. What could you choose?

Either could be a good choice! Buying the toy means having something now. Saving means you're one step closer to something bigger later. There's no wrong answer — it's your choice to make.

Looking ahead

What about money on a screen?

Once physical money makes sense, it's worth knowing that money can also be kept somewhere other than your pocket — in a bank account, shown as a number on a screen. A $1 coin, $1 in a bank account, and $1 on a screen are all the same real money, just kept in different places. That's a lesson for later, once coins and notes feel familiar.

For parents

You can reinforce this lesson in about five minutes, with five real $1 coins.

Give your child five $1 coins, then ask them to:

  1. Count the money.
  2. Choose something they could buy.
  3. Work out what they'd have left.
  4. Decide whether they'd rather spend it or save it.
