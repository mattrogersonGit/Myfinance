Getting Paid

When you do work for someone, they may pay you in return.

Good money practice

Counting your pay

Once you're paid, it's worth checking: how much did you actually receive? Does it match what was agreed? And what do you want to do with it? This isn't about not trusting someone — it's simply a good habit, every time money changes hands.

Before you start

Agreeing on payment

Before doing a paid job, it helps to agree on a few things first: what the job actually is, what needs to be done, how much will be paid, and when payment happens.

Example: "Wash the car properly and receive $10 when it is finished."

Agreeing this upfront prevents confusion later.

Not just cash

Payment doesn't always mean cash

For younger children, cash is the easiest place to start. But as people get older, they're often paid electronically — the money goes straight into a bank account instead of being handed over as coins and notes. We'll cover how that works later, in the Banks & Digital Money lessons.

A quick reminder

Earning vs receiving

Payment for work is earned money. A gift is received money. Getting paid is what happens right after you earn it.

Try it yourself

Before you start the job

You agree to mow the lawn for $15. What should you think about before starting?

What exactly needs doing? How much will you receive? When will you be paid? And what will you do with the money once you have it?

For parents

Before paying for larger jobs, agree clearly on what's expected. The goal isn't strict rules — it's teaching responsibility and the value of a clear agreement.

For older children

Some jobs are paid hourly, others are fixed-price for the whole task. Adults sometimes send an invoice — a simple note saying what work was done and how much is owed — before being paid electronically. This isn't about tax or employment law, just how getting paid tends to work.
