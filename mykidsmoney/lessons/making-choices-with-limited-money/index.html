Making Choices With Limited Money

You can't usually buy everything you want, so you get to decide what matters most.

The basics

Every dollar can only be used once

This isn't a lesson about not spending money — it's about understanding that whatever you have is what you have. Once you spend a dollar, it's spent.

$5You have $5. You could:
•spend $3 and save $2
•spend all $5
•save all $5
•give some away

Lots of choices — but only $5 to make them with.

Bringing it together

Spend, Save & Give

💸

Spend

Money you can use now.

🏺

Save

Money you keep for a future goal.

🎁

Give

Money you choose to give to help others.

You decide how to divide the money you actually have. There's no single correct split — a $2/$2/$1 example is just that, an example.

Try it yourself

A few real scenarios

1. You have $10

You want a $4 treat, a $7 toy, and you're saving towards $30 headphones. You can't do all three — what could you choose?

2. You have $5

You want something that costs $4. You could buy it now, or keep the $5 and keep saving. What might you choose?

3. You receive $5 pocket money

How might you divide it between Spend, Save and Give?

None of these have one correct answer — the point is thinking about choices and their consequences.

Connecting it all

Choices connect to goals

If you spend money today, you might take longer to reach your savings goal. If you save today, you might have to wait before buying something you want now. That's the trade-off from earlier lessons, showing up again here. And if money's limited, knowing whether something is a need or a want can help you decide what to prioritise first.

For parents

Give your child $10 in real $1 coins. Present a few choices, and ask them to physically divide the coins between Spend, Save and Give. Then ask:

  1. Why did you choose that?
  2. What did you decide not to buy?
  3. How much will you have saved?
  4. How long might it take to reach your goal?

This works best with real, limited money your child already has — pocket money or a small shopping budget is plenty. There's no need to invent an artificial situation.

An empowering idea

Choices, not restrictions

Money gives us choices. Having a limited amount just means deciding which choices matter most — it's not about deprivation.

For older children: this often comes down to prioritising — deciding what needs to happen first, what's most important, what can wait, and what you can actually afford.
